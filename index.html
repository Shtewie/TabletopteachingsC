Who is this for?

Most of the young people at our tables arrived for one of these three reasons.

“They'll read anything except a book.”

The reluctant reader

Reading a spell description to use it in a fight isn't homework — it's ammunition. Players read aloud, decode unfamiliar words and hunt for details in a clue because the story stops until they do.

“They go quiet in groups.”

The quiet one

Speaking as a character is easier than speaking as yourself. Turn order means everyone gets the floor and nobody has to fight for it, so quieter players build up to speaking without being put on the spot.

“They have a whole world in their head.”

The storyteller

Big imaginations often want somewhere structured to put it. Character backstories, in-world letters and session journals turn all that invention into actual writing practice — pages of it, willingly.

What actually happens in a session

A typical [[SESSION_LENGTH]] session. The learning goals are threaded through the story, not bolted on at the end.

  1. First 10 minutes

    Recap and settle in

    One player retells last session's events to the group. That's comprehension and sequencing practice, and it's the job everyone volunteers for.

  2. Next 20 minutes

    The hook

    A letter, a map, a set of instructions or an overheard conversation. Players read it aloud, work out what matters, and decide what to do about it.

  3. The main stretch

    Play

    Exploration, negotiation, puzzles and the occasional goblin. Every player speaks in turn, and the party has to agree on a plan before it happens — which is where the social skills get their workout.

  4. Last 15 minutes

    Write it down

    Journals out. A few sentences on what happened, a rhyme, a letter to an NPC, a name for the new sword. Short, low-pressure, and it goes home with them.

  5. After

    You get the update

    A note on what your child did at the table and which goals they moved forward — plus the take-home resource tied to the next session.

Before You Hand Over Your Kid

Fair questions. Here are the answers up front.

✓

Working With Children Check

All staff have a current Working With Children Check.

✓

Public liability insurance

Covered to $[[COVER]]. Certificate of currency available for schools and venues on request.

✓

Parents are welcome to stay

Sit in on any session, any time. Most parents stay for the first one and read a book in the corner for the rest.

✓

Age-appropriate content, always

Stories are written for the age group at the table, with agreed table rules and content everyone is comfortable with. Nothing gory, nothing frightening.

✓

We never publish players' names

The public adventure log uses character names only. No photos, surnames or identifying details go online without written parent consent.

✓

Small groups, one DM

Four to six players per table so nobody gets lost in the crowd, and so every player gets real airtime every session.

Questions Parents Ask

My child has never played D&D. Will they be lost?

No. Most players arrive having never touched a dice bag. The rules that matter get taught in the first ten minutes, and everything else is picked up in play. Character sheets are pre-built for new players so nobody spends their first session doing paperwork.

Where and when do sessions run?

Sessions run at [[VENUE]] in [[SUBURB]], [[SCHEDULE]]. Online sessions run over video for families outside the area, or for players who find a room full of people hard going.

Isn't this just a games club with a label on it?

Every campaign starts with a written list of reading, writing and social objectives for that group — you can see the live ones on the adventures page. Objectives are ticked off only when a player actually demonstrates them at the table, and you get a record of your own child's progress, not just the party's.

My kid is autistic / ADHD / anxious. Is this suitable?

Often it's a good fit — the structure of turn-taking and clear rules is exactly what makes it work for a lot of neurodivergent players. Tell us what helps and what doesn't when you get in touch, and we'll be honest with you about whether the group we have available is the right one.

What age groups do you run?

Ages 8 to 18, grouped into tables of similar age and reading level. Current groups are 8–10, 13–15 and 15–18. If your child sits outside a current group, tell us — new tables open when there's enough interest.

Do I have to commit to a whole term?

No. You can book a single session first to see how it lands. Most families move to a term campaign after that because the story — and the learning goals — build across sessions.

Is D&D too dark or scary for young kids?

Not the way we run it. Stories are written for the age at the table, agreed with parents, and follow table rules the players set themselves. The 8–10 campaign currently running is about relighting lanterns in a marsh and being polite to cats.

Can you run sessions at our school or service?

Yes. We run on-site blocks for schools, libraries, OOSH programs and support services, mapped to your literacy goals with written reporting on request. Use the form below and mention your organisation.

Ready to roll for initiative?

Tell us about your young adventurer — their age, their interests, and what you'd like them to get better at. We'll tell you honestly whether we've got the right table for them.
